# Historical Notes

## Scope and principle

The game follows a fictional British infantry section on the Western Front between 12 February 1917 and 11:00 on 11 November 1918. Public events have fixed dates and outcomes. The player can respond to them only at a personal scale.

The timeline is intentionally concise. It is context for a survival game, not a complete operational history. Dates are stored in UTC for deterministic software behaviour; displayed hours inside fictional scenes are dramatic local approximations unless the archive explicitly identifies a recorded zero hour.

## Recorded history represented in the game

| Record | Date used | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare | 1 February 1917 | Reaches soldiers through an old newspaper; it does not affect a land battle directly. |
| United States declares war on Germany | 6 April 1917 | News improves hope, while acknowledging that a large American field force cannot appear immediately. |
| Battle of Arras | 9 April–16 May 1917 | British route moves to the Arras sector; Vimy is mentioned as part of the wider offensive, not claimed as a British-only achievement. |
| Third Battle of Ypres | 31 July–10 November 1917 | The archive connects Passchendaele with the larger Flanders offensive and foregrounds destroyed drainage, rain, and mud. |
| Battle of Cambrai | 20 November–7 December 1917 | Described as a major, not exclusively tank, combined operation; early gains do not become a fictional breakthrough. |
| Eastern Front armistice | 15 December 1917 | Used to change trench rumours and concern over German transfers west. |
| Operation Michael | 21 March–5 April 1918 | The British section experiences broken communications and westward retreat; the player cannot halt the offensive. |
| Second Battle of the Marne | 15 July–6 August 1918 | Presented largely through reports because the fictional British section is not placed at the centre of a principally French-American context. |
| Hundred Days | 8 August–11 November 1918 | Begins at Amiens and changes the game from static trenches toward more mobile, logistically difficult fighting. |
| German political and military crisis | late October 1918 | Presented as uncertain newspaper and prisoner reports available to an ordinary soldier. |
| Armistice delegation and negotiations | from 7 November 1918 | Hope rises, but combat events remain possible because fighting continued during negotiations. |
| Armistice takes effect | 11:00, 11 November 1918 | The simulation ends at exactly 11:00. The player never “wins the war”; the guns simply stop. |

## Everyday material culture

Routine scenes draw on documented features of British service: front/support/rest rotation, stand-to, sentry duty, working and carrying parties, duckboards, field postcards and censored letters, small comforts such as tea and rum, mail, lice, rats, trench foot, battalion baths, gas drills, respirators, and long periods of monotony.

The starting **small box respirator** is deliberate: Imperial War Museums dates its introduction to August 1916 and notes that it became standard issue the following year. The game still makes correct use and timely warning matter.

## Fiction and synthesis

The following are wholly fictional:

- The player character and every generated service record.
- James Wilson, Arthur Hale, Edward Finch, George Bennett, Alfred Clarke, and all relatives.
- Every letter, personal diary sentence, conversation, local patrol, casualty, and postwar life.
- The section’s exact sequence of locations. It is a plausible dramatic route through British contexts, not a reconstruction of one named battalion’s war diary.
- Attribute numbers, risk coefficients, event weights, and all simulation outcome rates.

These people are not presented as hidden or forgotten historical individuals. UI labels explicitly mark personal records as generated fiction and public timeline notices as recorded history.

## Game simplifications and uncertainty

- A real soldier’s experience depended on unit, division, trade, wounds, leave, reinforcement schedules, and thousands of local circumstances. The game compresses this into roughly 55–110 decisions.
- Campaign start/end boundaries can differ among reference works according to whether a name covers an opening battle, a sequence of operations, or a later historiographical grouping. The selected ranges are documented, conventional boundaries suited to short archive entries.
- “Stress” is a gameplay pressure, not a retrospective diagnosis. The narrative uses observed experiences—fear, shaking, sleeplessness, panic, numbness—and avoids assigning a modern clinical label.
- The simulation’s death and survival percentages are balance outputs. They must not be read as a historical casualty rate for the British Army or any unit.
- A POW ending compresses captivity to a postscript; an invalided-home ending compresses treatment and discharge. Both are treated as survival endings without implying an easy experience.
- A postwar death year and life summary are fictional epilogue details derived from the seed, not historical prediction.
